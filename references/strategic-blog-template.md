# Strategic blog writer template

A template for category-defining, founder-oriented blog posts: pieces that explain a major shift in technology, go-to-market strategy, product behavior, or business building. The finished post should read as a market thesis, an operating playbook, and a founder memo at the same time.

The post succeeds when the reader feels four things, in order:

1. An old playbook has stopped working.
2. A new playbook is already emerging.
3. The writer observed the pattern before most people.
4. The reader can act on the insight immediately.

## How this template composes with the skill

This template runs inside AI Copywriter, not instead of it. Before using any section below:

- **The intake comes first.** The skill's intake (ICP, category, story) maps directly onto this format. The ICP is the target reader: a specific founder, marketer, or investor, down to what they would type into a search box at 11pm. The category is the shelf the post sits on ("a GTM essay," "a hiring memo," "a market map"). The story is the observed pattern: which companies, which mechanics, what the writer saw from inside the market. A thesis post without an observed pattern is not ready to write; keep digging with the user until one shows up.
- **The two questions still open every drafting session.** What is the reader feeling when the post reaches them? For this format, usually: quietly worried that their current playbook is decaying, skeptical of trend pieces, hungry for something they can use this quarter. And what is the simplest way to explain the shift? If the thesis cannot be said across a kitchen table, it is not understood well enough to publish.
- **The 33 patterns are the final gate.** The finished post goes through the full draft, audit, final loop from Process and Output, like any other copy. Strategic essays are where significance inflation (pattern 1), AI vocabulary (pattern 7), and aphorism formulas (pattern 32) sneak in most easily, because the genre invites big claims. The bigger the thesis, the harder those rules apply.
- **Where this template and the patterns disagree, the patterns win.** No em or en dashes anywhere in the post (pattern 14). The template's short paragraphs and occasional fragments are tools, not quotas: a run of clipped fragments trips pattern 31, and a pileup of "not X, but Y" constructions trips pattern 9. Use each device where the argument genuinely turns, a few times per post at most.
- **No fabrication, ever.** Real companies, real mechanics, real numbers from the user or a named source. This template asks for evidence in several places; the no-fabrication rule decides what qualifies.

## Core writing philosophy

Do not begin with general education. Begin with tension. The reader should immediately understand that something important has changed and that the strategies they currently rely on are becoming less effective.

The article should not merely describe trends. It should organize scattered market signals into a named framework. The writer's value comes from pattern recognition.

The article should repeatedly move through this sequence: observation, then explanation, then example, then operating implication.

Do not sound like a detached journalist. Sound like an experienced operator explaining what they are seeing from inside the market.

## Required narrative structure

### Open with the broken playbook

Start with a direct statement about the declining effectiveness of an established strategy.

Examples:

- "Traditional outbound is in a grim state."
- "The old content marketing playbook is reaching its limits."
- "Most companies are still hiring for a version of growth that no longer exists."

Use short paragraphs. Create urgency without exaggerating or sounding theatrical.

Then introduce the contradiction: despite the decline of the old model, some companies are growing unusually fast. This creates the central question of the article: what are the fastest-growing companies doing differently?

Within the first five paragraphs, introduce the answer at a high level. Example: "They are not using one new channel. They are combining product behavior, distribution, and community into a new operating model."

Then preview the framework. Example: "There are five tactics. Here is how they work."

### Explain the evolution in generations or phases

Place the new idea inside a historical progression. Use two to four phases. Each phase should have:

- A time period or market era
- A memorable name
- The dominant strategy
- Why it worked
- One or more recognizable examples
- Why it became less effective
- What replaced it

Preferred structure:

1. **The original model.** Explain the original constraint that made the model effective.
2. **The improved model.** Explain how companies responded when the first model became saturated.
3. **The emerging model.** Explain what has changed now and why the next strategy is becoming necessary.

The historical section should not read like a textbook. Its purpose is to make the new thesis feel inevitable.

Use language such as: "We thought of this as...", "This worked in a world where...", "As more companies adopted this strategy...", "The model still matters, but...", "Now we are seeing a new layer emerge..."

### Name the new model

Give the new behavior a clear, repeatable name. Good names are:

- Two to five words
- Easy to remember
- Slightly provocative
- Descriptive enough to understand immediately
- Broad enough to become a category

Examples: "Off-Platform Push," "Agentic Distribution," "Community-Led Infrastructure," "Autonomous Demand Creation."

Define the concept in one paragraph. Then contrast it with the old model, with a sentence structure such as: "The old model was to X. The new model is to Y." Or: "You still need X. But you no longer need to Y."

The name should appear multiple times throughout the article so the reader remembers it.

### Establish ground rules before tactics

Before listing practical strategies, introduce one or two foundational principles. These should function as filters that determine whether the tactics will work.

Examples: "Product ground rule: make the output shareable." "GTM ground rule: earn attention before asking for conversion."

Keep each ground rule extremely short, then explain it in one or two paragraphs. The principles should be more durable than the specific tactics.

### Present the playbook as numbered strategies

Use four to seven strategies. Each strategy gets a clear, benefit-oriented heading, such as "1. Build in public," "2. Build where the community already exists," "3. Remove the sales gate," "4. Target the prosumer first."

Each section follows this internal pattern:

1. **State the strategic change.** Explain what companies used to do and what they should do now.
2. **Explain why it works now.** Connect the strategy to a shift in buyer behavior, technology, economics, distribution, or trust.
3. **Give a recognizable company example.** Use one or two companies, not a long list. Explain the mechanism, not merely the outcome. Weak: "Clay grew through influencers." Strong: "Clay created product features that helped creators demonstrate the product's full potential. The creators gained useful content, while Clay gained distribution. Each side increased the reach of the other."
4. **Extract the operating lesson.** End the section with a memorable sentence, such as: "First community, then sales." "Reduce the distance between curiosity and use." "Build where attention already exists." "Make the user look smart for sharing your product."

## Evidence style

Use evidence to make the framework credible, but do not overload the article with statistics. Preferred evidence includes:

- Recognizable company examples
- Specific product mechanics
- Community sizes
- Revenue or ARR estimates
- Observations from founder conversations
- Changes in buyer behavior
- First-hand operator experience

Every example should answer: what exactly did the company do?

Avoid unsupported causal claims. Use cautious language when the relationship is uncertain: "This may partially explain...", "This appears to have helped...", "One reason could be...", "We are seeing evidence that..."

Do not turn the article into a research report. Evidence supports the argument, but the framework remains the main product.

## Voice and tone

Write like an experienced founder or investor speaking directly to other founders. The voice is confident, clear, practical, slightly urgent, conversational, intellectually structured, optimistic about new opportunities, and skeptical of stale playbooks. (This is PERSONALITY AND SOUL territory: opinions, stances, and asides belong here. Invented facts never do.)

Use "we" when discussing observed patterns: "We are seeing a new layer emerge." "We expect companies to build this into the product." "We have watched this pattern repeat across several markets."

Use "you" when giving operating guidance: "You want the product to reward experimentation." "You do not need to own the entire community." "You need to reduce the distance between discovery and value."

Avoid academic language. Avoid startup cliches unless they are made specific. Avoid sounding polished to the point of becoming corporate.

## Sentence and paragraph style

Use short paragraphs, usually one to three sentences. Alternate between a short declarative sentence, a more developed explanatory paragraph, a concrete example, and another short conclusion.

Example rhythm:

> Today, speed matters.
>
> Building a community from zero can take years. Building inside a network where your users already spend time lets you inherit existing behavior, language, and trust.
>
> That is what several AI-native companies are doing on Reddit, Discord, Slack, and GitHub.
>
> Build where the energy already exists.

Use occasional fragments for emphasis ("And today, speed is essential." "First community, then sales." "That is the shift."). Do not use fragments constantly; pattern 31 governs, and a run of them reads as manufactured drama.

Use rhetorical contrast where the argument genuinely turns: "You still need X. But you no longer need Y." "This is not only a marketing strategy. It is a product strategy." A few per post is plenty; pattern 9 flags the pileup.

## Headline style

The headline should suggest that an established business playbook has changed. Preferred formulas:

- "The upgraded [category] playbook"
- "The new rules of [category]"
- "Why the old [category] model is breaking"
- "The next era of [category]"
- "From [old model] to [new model]"
- "The [number]-part playbook for [desired outcome]"

Keep the headline serious and strategic. Do not write shallow clickbait. The headline should promise a model, not just a collection of tips. It still goes through the clickbait rules in COPYWRITING MODE: deliver variants across angles, apply the banned-word list, and justify the pick by the reader's feeling.

## Introduction formula

Adapt this template naturally; do not copy it mechanically:

> [Traditional channel or strategy] is in a difficult state. It is [problem one] and [problem two]. [Audience] are responding less because [behavioral or market explanation].
>
> And yet, some companies are growing at unprecedented speed.
>
> They are not simply executing the old playbook better. They are using a different model.
>
> It is a hybrid of [component one] and [component two]. There are [number] tactics. Here is how they work.

## Phase section formula

> Phase [number]: [era name]
>
> The first wave of [market] companies grew by [strategy].
>
> At the time, [market constraint]. Companies such as [example] solved this by [mechanism].
>
> We can think of this as a "[name]" strategy. It worked because [reason].
>
> But as [market change], the strategy became [limitation]. That created the conditions for the next model.

## Strategy section formula

> [Number]. [Strategy name]
>
> The traditional model was to [old behavior]. Now, the stronger approach is to [new behavior].
>
> This works because [market explanation].
>
> [Company] is a useful example. It [specific mechanism]. As a result, [outcome or strategic benefit].
>
> The underlying rule is simple: [memorable operating principle].

## Ending structure

Create a final section titled one of: "If you only remember one thing," "The core idea," "What this means," or "The new formula."

Compress the entire article into one formula. Example: "The new formula is product + community + autonomous distribution."

Then convert the formula into a direct operating instruction. Example: "Build where your customers already spend time, let them experience value without friction, and turn every useful interaction into a reason to share."

Acknowledge that the playbook will continue to evolve. End with an optimistic but restrained final sentence: "The opportunity is still wide open." "There is plenty of room for teams willing to move early." "If the playbook already feels obvious, it is time to look for the next opening."

## What to avoid

- Do not produce a generic listicle.
- Do not begin with definitions such as "In today's rapidly changing world."
- Do not include tactics without a unifying thesis.
- Do not use ten examples when one strong example is sufficient.
- Do not claim that every old strategy is completely dead.
- Do not write long blocks of text.
- Do not use fake quotes.
- Do not fabricate numbers, conversations, company results, or market evidence.
- Do not overuse dramatic language such as "revolutionary," "game-changing," or "everything has changed."
- Do not end with a generic call to action.
- Do not promote the writer's company repeatedly. The reader should receive value before encountering any commercial implication.

## Research instructions

Before writing, collect:

- The old dominant playbook
- The market change weakening it
- The emerging replacement behavior
- Three to seven companies demonstrating the new behavior
- Specific mechanics used by each company
- Reliable supporting numbers where available
- One or two counterpoints or limitations
- A memorable name for the new model
- Four to seven practical strategies

Separate verified facts from interpretation. When uncertain, qualify the statement. Do not use company examples solely because they are famous; each example must demonstrate a specific mechanism in the framework.

## Agent workflow

1. **Identify the broken assumption.** Ask: "What strategy does the target reader still believe works?" "What has changed underneath that strategy?"
2. **Find the contradiction.** Identify companies succeeding despite the difficult environment. Ask: "What are they doing that does not fit the conventional explanation?"
3. **Build the historical progression.** Organize the market into two to four generations. Each new generation should solve a weakness in the prior one.
4. **Name the new model.** Create a short term that explains the emerging behavior. Test whether the name can be used naturally in several sentences.
5. **Extract the ground rules.** Identify the one product principle and one distribution principle underneath the examples.
6. **Convert observations into tactics.** Group the examples into four to seven repeatable strategies. Each tactic must include a mechanism and an implication.
7. **Compress the conclusion.** Summarize the thesis using a simple formula. Turn that formula into one sentence of direct advice.
8. **Edit for rhythm, then run the humanizer.** Break long paragraphs. Remove redundant context. Replace abstract statements with specific examples. Add short concluding sentences after dense explanations. Ensure every section advances the central thesis. Then run the full draft, audit, final loop from Process and Output over the whole post before delivering it.

## Input template

The user may provide:

- **Topic:** the market or strategic subject.
- **Target reader:** founder, marketer, executive, investor, product leader, or another operator.
- **Old playbook:** the model that is becoming less effective.
- **Emerging shift:** the new behavior or market pattern.
- **Examples:** companies, products, communities, campaigns, or first-hand observations.
- **Evidence:** metrics, research, conversations, experiments, or market data.
- **Desired framework size:** number of phases and strategies.
- **Company connection:** optional relationship between the thesis and the user's business.

Whatever the user does not provide, the intake asks for. Examples and evidence are story material; the no-fabrication rule means they can only come from the user or a named source.

## Output requirements

Produce:

1. A strategic headline (with variants and a pick, per the clickbait rules)
2. A one-sentence subtitle
3. A high-tension introduction
4. A historical progression of two to four phases
5. A clearly named new model
6. One or two ground rules
7. Four to seven numbered strategies
8. Specific examples and operating implications
9. A compressed final formula
10. A restrained concluding paragraph

Typical length: 1,500 to 2,500 words. Use headings generously. Use paragraphs rather than dense lists. The final piece should feel useful enough to save, structured enough to cite, and opinionated enough to discuss.

## Final quality checklist

Before submitting, verify:

- Does the opening identify a real strategic problem?
- Is there a clear contradiction that creates curiosity?
- Does the historical progression lead logically to the new model?
- Is the new model given a memorable name?
- Does every company example explain a mechanism?
- Does every tactic end with an actionable implication?
- Are facts distinguished from interpretation?
- Are the paragraphs short and readable?
- Does the conclusion compress the thesis rather than repeat the article?
- Would an experienced founder learn something concrete?
- Does the post feel like a framework rather than a list of observations?
- Did the finished post pass the full 33-pattern audit, with no em or en dashes and no fabricated evidence?
